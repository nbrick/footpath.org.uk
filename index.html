footpath.org.uk
About this site

A shortcut, nothing more. A blocked path is reported to the highway authority for the area it runs through, and working out which one that is tends to be the slowest part. This site does that bit and then gets out of the way.

Why the right one matters. The duty to keep a path clear doesn’t sit with councils in general — section 130 of the Highways Act 1980 puts it on one named highway authority and nobody else. A report that reaches the wrong council isn’t merely slow; it has arrived somewhere with no power to act on it. Hence a whole site for one lookup.

Independent. Not affiliated with any council, and built in the open on GitHub.

Report a blocked public right of way

Footpaths, bridleways and byways are kept open by the highway authority for the area the path runs through — usually a county or unitary council.

Scotland works differently

Not a gap in this list — a different system. The Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 gives a right of responsible access to most land and water, rather than recording particular routes on a definitive map. Every Scottish council, plus the Cairngorms and Loch Lomond & The Trossachs park authorities, is an access authority with a statutory duty to uphold that right, so an obstruction goes to that authority’s access officer. NatureScot’s guide to your access rights explains what the right covers.

Northern Ireland works differently

Not covered here. Public rights of way are the responsibility of the 11 district councils under the Access to the Countryside (Northern Ireland) Order 1983, which requires them to protect and maintain routes and to keep maps of them. Far fewer paths are legally recorded than in Great Britain, so a path in regular use may have no recorded status at all. nidirect’s guide to public rights of way is the clearest starting point; your district council’s countryside access team is who to contact.

No authority by that name here. Check the spelling, or search that council’s own website for “report a problem with a public right of way”.

Worth having ready before you click through

A grid reference or what3words for the exact spot, the path number if you can find it (look it up on rowmaps.com), and a photo or two. It’s what the authority needs to act on it.

Why the authority has to listen

Acting on this isn’t discretionary. Under section 130 of the Highways Act 1980 a highway authority has a duty to “assert and protect the rights of the public to the use and enjoyment” of its highways, and to prevent them being stopped up or obstructed. Wilfully obstructing free passage along one is an offence in its own right under section 137. Which routes count is settled by the definitive map, kept under Part III of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 — a path on that map stays a right of way whether or not anyone has walked it lately. GOV.UK has a plain-English overview, though it covers England only.

A complement to the Ramblers, not a replacement

The Ramblers do the long-term work — campaigning to keep paths open and researching lost ones back onto the definitive map. Their report a path problem guide is the fuller resource, covering escalation and how to record paths that were never mapped. This site does one narrow thing by comparison: get you from a blocked path to the right authority’s form as quickly as possible.
